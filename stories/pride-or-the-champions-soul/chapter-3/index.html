Chapter 3
The Overture

In the half-light of the carriage, an ōgi board emerged from the folds of the blue kimono. The pieces, carved from an ancient wood, caught the silver glints filtering through the windows. Their intricate patterns seemed almost alive in the shifting light, as if animated by a will of their own.

The proposal came in a barely audible murmur, an invitation to play tinged with studied deference. Takeshi watched the stranger's hands set out the pieces with a precision that betrayed an unusual familiarity with the game. Her fingers brushed each piece as though they already knew its destiny on the ōgiban.

A refusal rose to Takeshi's lips, automatic, reasonable. Tomorrow's tournament demanded his full attention. Yet something in the way she handled the pieces aroused his curiosity. Her gestures suggested a mastery that went beyond mere knowledge of the rules—a deep understanding of the very soul of the game.

She was speaking now of her own path, of how she had learned ōgi, her voice as fluid as the movements of her hands over the board. Her words wove a subtle web, in which sincere admiration mingled with a precise knowledge of his most memorable games.

The champion felt his resolve waver. His mind, trained to analyse moves and intentions, detected in this encounter something that went beyond mere coincidence. At last, with a gesture marked by that benevolent condescension peculiar to masters, he picked up his rook—the centrepiece of his aggressive style—and removed it from the board. This handicap, offered with a barely perceptible smile, was as much a mark of courtesy as an assertion of his superiority.

An imperceptible smile crossed the woman's face, as fleeting as a pale reflection on water. In the half-light of the carriage, that smile seemed to carry a deeper meaning than the mere acknowledgement of an unexpected generosity.
